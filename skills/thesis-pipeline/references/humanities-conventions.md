# Humanities research conventions

This pipeline is built for argument-driven research at masters level: a thesis
defended through reading, analysis and reasoning rather than through collected
data. It is subject-agnostic within that: philosophy, history, literature,
theology, classics, art history, musicology, cultural and area studies,
linguistics, law where doctrinal, and interdisciplinary work of the same shape.

## Primary and secondary sources

The distinction structures everything and differs by field:

| Field | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| History | Archive documents, letters, records, newspapers of the period | Historians writing about them |
| Literature | The novel, poem, play, manuscript | Criticism of it |
| Philosophy | The philosopher's own text | Commentary and interpretation |
| Art history | The work, sketch, catalogue | Art-historical writing |
| Theology | Scripture, liturgy, doctrinal statements | Theological scholarship |

Mark every literature note `type: primary` or `type: secondary`. They are read
differently, cited differently, and carry different weight: a claim about what
an author says is settled by the primary text, not by a commentator's report of
it.

## Editions, translations and versions

In humanities work, *which* copy is cited is part of the citation, not a detail.

- **Editions differ.** Pagination, included material and sometimes the text
  itself vary between editions. Record the edition used, and use one edition
  throughout for a given work.
- **Translations are interpretations.** Name the translator. Where a claim turns
  on a particular word, say which translation and consider noting the original
  term.
- **Historical texts carry two dates.** Original composition or publication, and
  the edition consulted: `(Author, 1844/1959)`. Follow the project's style for
  the exact form.
- **Standard reference systems override page numbers** for many canonical texts:
  Stephanus numbers for Plato, Bekker for Aristotle, book and line for epic and
  drama, manuscript or folio numbers for archival material, article and section
  for legal instruments. Use the system the field expects; a page number from a
  particular printing is the weakest possible locator.

## Archival material

Where the user works with archives, record for each item: repository,
collection, box or shelfmark, item description, and date. Ask for these at note
creation; they cannot be reconstructed later. Flag `[UNVERIFIED]` on any
reference the user has from notes rather than from the item itself.

## Close reading and quotation

Humanities argument runs on quotation, which raises the stakes on the quotation
gate:

- Quote exactly, including original spelling and punctuation, marking changes
  with square brackets and omissions with ellipses
- Long quotations follow the project's style for block formatting
- Never paraphrase a passage the argument turns on: quote it and analyse it
- Where a translation is quoted, the translator is part of the attribution

## The shape of the argument

A humanities thesis usually moves: territory and question, definitions and
scope, the position being engaged, the critical apparatus, the argument applied,
objections and replies, conclusion. There is no methods chapter in the empirical
sense; there is a statement of approach, and it belongs in the introduction.

Two things markers look for that this pipeline should protect:

- **Positioning.** What conversation is this entering, and what does it add?
  The rival check and the literature notes supply this.
- **Charity.** Opponents stated at their strongest before being answered. When
  drafting an objection, write the version its holder would recognise.

## Masters-level scope

A masters dissertation is typically 10,000 to 20,000 words and defends one
claim well. Guard against the commonest scope failure: a question that would
need a doctorate. When the thesis grows, say so, and offer the narrower version
alongside it.
